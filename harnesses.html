Climbing harnesses

A harness is safety-critical gear, so fit, comfort and condition matter. Here’s how to choose one and what to check.

What to look for

Fit and comfort

The waist belt sits above your hips and stays put when you hang. Try it on with a bit of weight on it, if you can.

Adjustability

Adjustable leg loops let you fit layers of clothing, and make it easier to share between climbers.

Gear loops

More loops help when you carry gear. A gym harness needs very few.

Weight and padding

Lighter harnesses suit sport climbing, and more padding is more comfortable on long routes.

Types of harness

All-round

A comfortable, adjustable harness that works in the gym and outdoors.

Sport

Lighter and more minimal, built for hard single-pitch climbing.

Trad or multi-pitch

More padding and gear loops for carrying equipment and hanging for longer.

Safety firstFollow the manufacturer’s instructions, learn from a qualified instructor and inspect your harness for wear before every use. Retire it according to the manufacturer’s guidance, and never use one with an unknown history.

Which one for your level

Beginner

A comfortable all-round harness, and have an instructor show you how to put it on and tie in.

Intermediate

A lighter sport harness if you climb outdoors, with a few gear loops.

Advanced

A specialist harness for the type of climbing you do most.

Tips before you buy

  • Roped climbing needs a harness, but bouldering does not.
  • Check the labels for safety certification marks.
  • Try the harness on over the clothes you will climb in.
  • Ask your gym or a coach to check the fit with you.

Common mistakes

  • Choosing the wrong size, or buying one that is too loose or too tight.
  • Skipping regular checks for wear on the webbing and buckles.
  • Not learning how to fasten and check it properly.
  • Buying a second-hand harness when you don’t know its history.
