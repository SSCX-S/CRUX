Introduction to Climbing

A beginner’s guide to the sport: what it involves, how grades work and the core techniques to learn first.

Beginner4 min read

01What is climbing?

Climbing is a sport that involves moving across or upwards on natural rock formations or artificial climbing walls. It combines physical strength, coordination, balance, problem-solving and mental focus.

Unlike many traditional sports, climbing requires athletes to think carefully about each movement. Every hold, foothold and body position can influence how efficiently a climber completes a route.

Climbing is also a highly accessible sport. People of different ages, experience levels and physical abilities can participate, with routes designed to accommodate beginners through to advanced athletes.

There are several major forms of climbing:

Bouldering

Climbing shorter walls without ropes, using thick padded mats for protection.

Sport climbing

Climbing taller routes while attached to a rope and using fixed protection points.

Traditional climbing

Climbers place their own removable protection while ascending natural rock.

Speed climbing

Racing against another climber or the clock on a standardised route.

Where to startFor beginners, indoor bouldering is often a convenient introduction because it requires relatively little equipment and allows climbers to practise fundamental movements.

02Understanding climbing grades

Climbing grades are used to communicate the difficulty of a route or problem.

In many indoor bouldering gyms, the V-scale is used. It begins at V0 and increases as routes become more challenging. For example:

  1. V0–V1

    Beginner-friendly problems that introduce basic climbing movements.

  2. V2–V3

    More demanding problems requiring improved balance and coordination.

  3. V4–V5

    Intermediate problems that introduce more complex movements and physical challenges.

  4. V6–V8

    Advanced problems requiring refined technique, strength and problem-solving.

  5. V9 and above

    Highly challenging problems generally suited to experienced climbers.

Grades are not universalDifferent gyms, setters and climbing styles can produce noticeably different difficulties. A climber should focus on improving their movement and consistency rather than comparing grades with other people.

03Essential climbing techniques

Good climbing technique is about using your body efficiently. Beginners sometimes rely heavily on their arms, but experienced climbers understand that footwork, balance and body positioning are equally important.

Footwork

Footwork is one of the most important foundations of climbing. Rather than placing your entire foot against a wall, climbing shoes allow you to use specific areas of the shoe to stand on small holds. Some basic footwork techniques include:

  • EdgingUsing the edge of your climbing shoe to stand on a foothold.
  • SmearingPressing the rubber sole against the wall when there is no obvious foothold.
  • Toe placementPositioning the front of your shoe accurately on a hold.
  • Quiet feetPlacing your feet deliberately and avoiding unnecessary movement.

Accurate foot placement helps maintain balance and reduces wasted energy.

Three points of contact

Three points of contact is a useful beginner principle. It involves maintaining three points of contact with the climbing wall while moving one limb at a time.

For example, you might keep both feet and one hand on holds while moving your other hand. This encourages controlled movement and helps beginners avoid unnecessary swinging.

A guideline, not a ruleDynamic climbing movements sometimes require several limbs to move together.

Body positioning

Your body position determines how effectively you can reach holds and maintain balance. Important principles include:

  • Keep your hips reasonably close to the wall when appropriate.
  • Avoid unnecessarily pulling yourself away from the wall.
  • Maintain a comfortable and balanced stance.
  • Bend your knees when useful to reach lower holds.
  • Rotate your hips and shoulders to improve reach.

Good positioning can make a difficult-looking movement considerably easier.

Weight distribution

Climbers need to distribute their weight effectively between their hands and feet. Beginners often place too much weight on their arms, causing their upper body to become tired quickly.

Instead, practise standing through your feet and allowing your legs to support much of your body weight. Learning to shift your centre of gravity is particularly useful when moving between holds.
