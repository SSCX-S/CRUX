Crash pads

A crash pad softens your landing when you boulder outdoors. Size, portability and landing zone matter more than any single feature.

What to look for

Size and thickness

Bigger pads cover more of the landing zone. Thicker pads soften bigger falls, but no pad removes the risk.

Foam

Pads often use several layers of foam. A firmer top layer spreads impact, and softer layers underneath absorb it.

Fold style

Taco, hinge or bi-fold. This affects how it closes, carries and covers the ground.

Carry system

Padded shoulder straps and a hip belt matter when you walk to a boulder.

Types of pad

Taco

Folds like a taco and closes with buckles. Compact, and it protects the foam.

Hinge or bi-fold

Folds flat in half, which gives an even landing surface.

Small or sit-start pad

Good for low problems and for adding coverage next to a bigger pad.

Safety firstA pad does not replace spotting, good landing technique or sensible choices about height. Check the ground for rocks, roots and gaps, and go with experienced climbers while you learn.

Which one for your level

Beginner

One mid-sized pad plus a spotter, and stay on low problems while you learn to fall.

Intermediate

A bigger pad or two, so you can cover the landing zone on higher problems.

Advanced

Several pads, and the skill to arrange them carefully on uneven ground.

Tips before you buy

  • Think about how far you will walk to the boulders, and pick a pad you can carry.
  • Look at how the pad folds and whether it closes securely.
  • Consider borrowing or hiring one before you buy.
  • Look after your pad and check it for wear.

Common mistakes

  • Using one small pad for a tall problem.
  • Leaving gaps between pads or placing them over rocks and roots.
  • Climbing without a spotter.
  • Forgetting that a pad doesn’t protect against every fall.
