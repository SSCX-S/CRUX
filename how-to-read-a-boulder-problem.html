How to read a boulder problem

A simple routine for working out a problem before you pull on, so every attempt counts.

Beginner4 min read

01Why reading matters

Reading a boulder problem means studying it before you pull on. A few minutes on the floor can save energy and attempts, because you arrive at the hard move with a plan and not a guess.

It also makes falling useful. When you know what you were trying to do, you can tell whether the plan failed or the execution did.

02The six-step routine

Use this routine on any problem. It takes about a minute once you’re used to it.

  1. Find the start and the finishCheck the tags or tape that mark the problem. Note where both hands start and what counts as finishing. Gym rules vary, so ask staff if you’re unsure.
  2. Pick out your hand holdsTrace the hand holds from start to finish. Tape or colour usually shows which holds belong to the problem.
  3. Check your feetLook for foot holds, and check whether your gym allows any feet or only marked ones. Footholds often hold the answer to a hard move.
  4. Spot the cruxThe crux is the hardest move. It usually sits where holds get smaller, further apart, or the wall gets steeper.
  5. Find the restsLook for jugs or balanced positions where you can shake out and breathe before the crux.
  6. Plan your sequenceDecide which hand goes where, which foot follows, and where your hips need to turn. Trace it in the air before you leave the floor.

A quick exampleA problem starts on big jugs, has a small crimp in the middle and finishes on a sloper. The crimp is likely the crux. Plan to rest on the last jug before it, and decide how you’ll set your feet for the sloper.

03Reading hold types

Knowing what a hold is tells you how to use it. Learn to recognise these five:

Jug

A large, positive hold you can wrap your hand around. Usually your best place to rest.

Crimp

A small edge for your fingertips. Hard on the fingers, so move onto it with care.

Sloper

A rounded hold with no edge. It works through friction, body position and tension.

Pinch

A hold you squeeze between your thumb and fingers.

Pocket

A hole for one to three fingers. Warm up well before pulling on small pockets.

Also notice which way each hold wants you to pull: down, sideways, or up as an undercling. Match your body position to that direction and the move often gets easier.

04Planning your sequence

This plan is often called “beta”. Start from the crux and work backwards: where do you want to be before it, and which hand and foot do you need there? Then work forwards from the start so everything lines up.

Look for places to switch or match your hands, turn your hips and set your feet. If you’re unsure, the trick is to test two ideas, not just one.

Try thisTrace the sequence in the air with your hands and feet while standing on the floor. If you run out of holds or lose balance, change the plan before you climb.

05When the plan doesn’t work

Most plans need adjusting. When an attempt fails, change one thing at a time: the hand you use, where your foot goes, or the way your hips are turned. Changing everything at once makes it hard to see what helped.

Watching other climbers is a good way to see different ideas, and asking for tips is part of climbing culture. Try the problem yourself first, so you learn to read it. Filming an attempt also shows details you can’t feel on the wall.

06Common mistakes

  • Rushing at the wall without a plan.
  • Only looking at hand holds and ignoring feet.
  • Stopping your plan at the crux, with no idea what comes after it.
  • Repeating the same failed move instead of changing one thing.
  • Never checking how the finish should look.

07A practice drill

Choose three problems at a comfortable grade. For each one, read it from the floor and say your plan out loud: start, crux, rest and finish. Then climb it and compare what happened with your plan. Over a few weeks, your reading will get faster and more accurate.
